Typical neurons consist of three components: dendrites, soma, and axons. These together follow follow a standard structure of input (dendrites collect input from other neurons), processing (soma perform the spike-generating operations on the input signal), and output (axons transmit the signal to other neurons). The junction between two neurons is called the synapse, across which the transmission occurs, and an axon may have multiple of these, mostly within the neighbourhood of the neuron. The spike-generation performed in the soma is the crucial unit of computation in the brain.

Neurotrophic electrodes along with amplifiers are used to measure membrane and action potentials inside neurons, where spikes have been observed to be short pulses (with magnitudes of around 100 mV, lasting 1-2 ms). Since any given neuron produces the same form of spikes, the shape of these potential does not contain information, but instead the rates and timing of spikes are what matter. Multiple spikes on an axon path are separated by an absolute refractory period at minimum, which is usually always significant enough to distinctly identify spikes.

Synapses can be classified as either chemical (synaptic clefts) or electrical (gap junctions). In the former, the axon terminals come very close to the post-synaptic neuron, and at the site neurotransmitters are released into the cleft upon an action potential arriving. The dendrites (or even soma) at the post-synaptic end receive these chemicals and translate them again to electric responses (again, as spikes). Gap junctions instead rely on special proteins to make direct electric conduction between neurons.

The structure of neurons is optimised to be both reduce connection delay and the amount of bio-material required for the wiring, both of which are separate objectives with most neurons lying on a Pareto optimal front. The simplest neuron structure is where all dendrites are directly connected to the soma, which is the same as a perceptron model. However, a neuron's dendritic tree can contain multiple branches and be modelled with trees or graphs with eventual connection to the soma. $k$-tree models have hierachical stractures where at eahc layer the tree branches into $k$ with leaf nodes being that actual contact points, while fully-connected models represent more of a graph or an ANN's layer. In simulation, computational performance increases with larger values of $k$ and with larger fully connected networks, but when using more realistic models where the distribution of branches and connections is much more assyemtric reduces this performance.
